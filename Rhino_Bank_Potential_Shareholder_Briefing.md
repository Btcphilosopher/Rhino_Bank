# RHINO BANK — POTENTIAL SHAREHOLDER BRIEFING

**Date:** 16 November 2026  
**Subject:** An estimated $100 billion equity valuation in 2026, with exposure to the long-term potential of global synthetic finance  
**Basis:** Investor scenario. The $100bn starting valuation is the sponsor's stipulated estimate, interpreted as total equity value; it is not an independently verified market valuation. All 2035 projections and valuation multiples are illustrative.

**The proposition for shareholders**

Rhino Bank's investment case is ownership of a commercial institution that could become a major organiser of global synthetic finance. Starting from an assumed equity valuation of $100bn in mid-to-late 2026, shareholders would participate in the growth of a business earning fees from origination, CDO structuring, credit-risk distribution, portfolio administration and servicing.

The strategic horizon is a 2035 financing architecture administering $200tn of average outstanding unique reference obligations. In the preceding operating model, that scale supports $280bn of annual net fee revenue and $142bn of EBITA. The shareholder opportunity lies in the earnings and equity value that could emerge from that franchise.

This is a terminal ambition, with no probability of attainment assigned. No current audited earnings, loan book, capital position, share count, licence evidence or investor commitments have been supplied. The assumed $100bn valuation therefore serves as the entry-price benchmark for the briefing rather than a conclusion supported by a current financial appraisal.

**What an investor owns**

An ordinary shareholder would own a residual interest in Rhino Bank, subject to its actual share class and constitutional documents. Potential returns come from dividends and appreciation in that interest. Rhino's platform, contracts, data capabilities and institutional relationships would support the franchise; investors in its issued CDOs would own the securities they purchase.

The $100bn equity valuation is distinct from cash in the bank, book equity, regulatory capital, total assets and lendable funds. A secondary purchase transfers money to a selling shareholder; a primary subscription provides new capital to Rhino and changes the share count. Any actual offering must specify whether the quoted valuation is pre-money or post-money and what rights accompany the shares.

**How synthetic finance creates commercial value**

Rhino would connect borrowers and originating lenders with investors willing to hold defined credit exposures. Funded loans and cash securitisations provide or refinance borrower funding. Synthetic CDOs and related credit instruments distribute specified risks. RhinoQuant supplies pricing, stress analysis and ongoing portfolio monitoring.

Rhino's revenue can grow with the volume of contracts it arranges and services. The franchise becomes more valuable if institutions repeatedly use its standards, valuations and execution systems. Recurring servicing revenue could complement transaction fees and strengthen customer relationships.

The $200tn figure measures unique reference principal, excluding duplicate synthetic overlays. It does not imply that Rhino owns $200tn of assets, raises $200tn of fresh cash or receives the returns on every referenced obligation. Those distinctions make the earnings case assessable.

**The 2035 full-scale earnings case**

| Driver | Assumption | Annual amount |
|---|---|---:|
| Average reference portfolio | $200tn | — |
| Annual arrangements | 20% of average portfolio | $40tn |
| Administration and servicing | 10bp net fee on average principal | $200bn |
| Structuring and distribution | 20bp net fee on annual arrangements | $80bn |
| **Net fee revenue** | After contractual partner shares and pass-through amounts | **$280bn** |
| Variable operating expenses | 30% of revenue | ($84bn) |
| Fixed cash operating expenses | Assumed | ($30bn) |
| Depreciation | Assumed | ($4bn) |
| Risk planning charge | Assumed; not a calibrated accounting provision | ($20bn) |
| **Modelled EBITA** | After the above expenses | **$142bn** |

EBITA excludes amortisation, corporate financing interest and tax. The operating model assumes no material additional net funding, warehousing or hedge costs beyond those reflected in its net revenue and expenses. Actual additional costs would reduce earnings. Protection premiums payable to risk-bearing investors are not Rhino fee income.

At this scale, one basis point on average reference principal is $20bn annually. Pricing and cost discipline can materially affect returns even when the changes look small in percentage terms.

**Translating earnings into equity value**

For a shareholder valuation illustration, assume the $142bn EBITA is reduced by a further $12bn of combined amortisation and corporate financing interest. That leaves $130bn of pretax earnings. An assumed 25% effective tax rate produces $97.5bn of net earnings attributable to ordinary shareholders, assuming no further preferred claims or minority interests. The additional deductions and tax rate are modelling assumptions, not verified forecasts.

Applying illustrative price-to-earnings multiples directly to those net earnings gives:

| Assumed 2035 P/E | Illustrative 2035 equity value | Multiple of $100bn entry valuation | Annualised appreciation over nine years |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 12× | $1.17tn | 11.7× | 31.4% |
| 16× | $1.56tn | 15.6× | 35.7% |
| 20× | $1.95tn | 19.5× | 39.1% |

These multiples are selected sensitivities, with no claim that they reflect current market comparables. They value ordinary equity through hypothetical net earnings, rather than treating an EBITA-based enterprise value as equity value. The returns exclude dividends, transaction costs and investor taxes and assume the investor's ownership is undiluted. They are not probability-weighted expected returns or present fair values.

The reference illustration is therefore a potential increase from $100bn to $1.56tn of equity value if the full operating case, earnings bridge and 16× terminal multiple all hold.

**Partial success and downside**

The opportunity should be evaluated at smaller scales as well as at universal adoption. The following cases reuse the earlier model's fee schedule and scaled operating-cost assumptions. They relocate its selected operating scales to alternative 2035 outcomes rather than predicting a year-by-year growth path.

| Alternative 2035 outcome | Smaller franchise | Broad franchise | Full-scale ambition |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Average reference portfolio | $20tn | $65tn | $200tn |
| Net fee revenue | $28bn | $91bn | $280bn |
| EBITA | $7.8bn | $40.7bn | $142bn |
| Additional amortisation and corporate financing interest | $2bn | $5bn | $12bn |
| Net earnings at 25% tax | $4.35bn | $26.775bn | $97.5bn |
| Assumed P/E | 12× | 14× | 16× |
| **Illustrative equity value** | **$52.2bn** | **$374.85bn** | **$1.56tn** |
| Multiple of $100bn entry | 0.52× | 3.75× | 15.6× |
| Annualised appreciation over nine years | −7.0% | 15.8% | 35.7% |

The smaller case assumes $9bn fixed cash expenses and $0.8bn depreciation; the middle case assumes $15bn and $1.5bn respectively. All cases assume variable expenses of 30% of net revenue and a risk planning charge of 1bp of reference principal. These costs would require validation for each scale. The differing P/E assumptions are illustrative, not calibrated to risk.

A successful operating business can still produce a disappointing investment when its entry valuation already anticipates substantial growth. The smaller franchise above would administer enormous reference volumes yet be worth less than the stipulated entry price under these assumptions. Failure, insolvency or a severe recapitalisation could cause substantially greater losses, including loss of the entire equity investment.

**What a shareholder's stake could become**

For illustration, a $1bn secondary investment at a $100bn equity valuation acquires 1% before costs, assuming equal economic rights and no other securities. At the $1.56tn terminal equity value, that stake would be worth $15.6bn if its percentage ownership remains unchanged. If subsequent issuance reduces that ownership to 0.5%, the holding would instead be worth $7.8bn at the same terminal equity value. New capital may help build the business, but shareholders must assess the resulting dilution alongside the growth it enables.

Net earnings also do not equal dividends. Regulatory capital, liquidity buffers, investment and retained exposures can require substantial earnings retention. No dividend payout is forecast here.

**The World Bank as an institutional benchmark**

The World Bank provides a useful benchmark for international reach, institutional confidence and the mobilisation of capital. It comprises IBRD and IDA; the wider group includes other institutions. IBRD's FY26 annual report records $50.2bn of new lending commitments. Its shareholders are member governments, which govern the institution. [1][2]

Rhino's proposed commercial potential is a global fee franchise available to its own shareholders, assuming the relevant ownership structure and permissions. The World Bank's potential is expressed through development finance and its member mandate. A private investor's Rhino equity claim has different economics from World Bank bonds or sovereign membership.

By 2035, Rhino could administer much larger reference volumes than the World Bank's current lending flows while still having a smaller own balance sheet. Institutional credibility, resilience and financial resources would need to be established separately. No World Bank 2035 size projection or World Bank/IMF commitment to purchase Rhino instruments is assumed.

**The conditions that make the upside investable**

The shareholder thesis depends on sustainable fee contracts, repeat investor demand, enforceable loss settlement and limited retained risks that Rhino can actually finance. A 1% pro rata retained interest in a $200tn reference portfolio equals $2tn of exposure; a 10% net loss on it would cost $200bn. Retained first-loss exposure can be substantially more severe. The earnings model cannot justify unlimited guarantees.

The larger the network becomes, the stronger the likely demands for service continuity, independent governance, transparent pricing and limits on concentration. These could build trust and support adoption while also increasing costs or compressing fees. A universal franchise is therefore compatible with immense scale and still requires a disciplined estimate of what shareholders can retain.

**Shareholder assessment**

At an assumed $100bn entry equity valuation, Rhino Bank offers exposure to a potentially vast expansion in synthetic-finance infrastructure. The full 2035 scenario supports an illustrative equity value of $1.17tn–$1.95tn under the selected earnings and valuation assumptions, with $1.56tn as the central sensitivity. Partial adoption produces a much wider range of outcomes.

The investment case rests on converting global financial activity into durable, capital-aware earnings. Evidence of signed business, realised fees, audited performance, funded risk capacity and ownership terms would determine how much of that future potential can reasonably be paid for today.
